00 / NOT A RESCUE STORY

THIS DID NOT START AS ADVOCACY

This actually started in 2011, in my primary-care physician's office.

I was diagnosed with depression. My physician prescribed me tramadol 50 mg for it. The same physician also prescribed 0.25 mg alprazolam for anxiety.

That is where my written drug history begins.

Not heroin. Not a methadone clinic. Not Suboxone. Not Medi-Cal. Not CalAudit.

A prescription for tramadol for depression.

Oxycodone and Oxycontin later entered the mix from a relative stuck in pain management for unexplained body pain. They were safe pharmaceuticals. They came from doctors and pharmacies, after all. Then suddenly one day out of the blue they became polysubstance abuse.

By 2013, alprazolam had become an addiction.

In 2014, my benzodiazepine use escalated to approximately 22 mg of alprazolam per day for about eight months. It ended in a grand mal seizure. I remember it took about six months of benzo belly and feeling every single drop of water in the shower to even start to think about feeling regular again.

The older intake history describes the drug I used in 2015 as Fentanyl. My current recollection is more specific: it was Acetylfentanyl. I grew tired of hearing huh? when I said Acetyl so I just put down Fentanyl whenever asked.

Heroin came later, after Jose number two passed away. Heroin was not the starting point.

THE PAPER TRAIL DID NOT START THE PROBLEM.
IT EVENTUALLY STARTED RECORDING IT.

This is not a chronology I reconstructed later for CalAudit. I had already written it down and submitted it as part of my medical intake history.

THE DRUG HISTORY WAS ALREADY ON PAPER BEFORE CALAUDIT EXISTED.

I already expected Medi-Cal to be bad. I expected limited networks, bureaucracy, long waits and the normal administrative trash.

What surprised me was the extent of it: how many different systems could touch the same problem without any one of them actually owning it, and how much documentation those systems would create while doing it.

CalAudit did not begin because I decided to become a get along activist. It began because I noticed something simpler.

EVERY TIME I TOUCHED THE MACHINE, THE MACHINE LEFT SOMETHING BEHIND.

A letter. A medical note. A call log. A referral. A denial. A claim. A grievance. A portal message. A prescription record.

I did not begin by creating evidence. The systems created it themselves.

01 / THE FIRST DOMINO

THE STORY STARTS BEFORE THE PRESCRIPTION RECORD

I had already been receiving methadone treatment at Pacific Western.

After months in the program, I was discharged following a dispute over whether an emergency-room visit after a missed dose amounted to a double-dose violation. I believed the underlying calendar showed something different.

So I asked for my medical chart.

I was told I needed a subpoena to get it.

I WAS NOT TRYING TO BUILD A CASE STUDY. I WAS TRYING TO STAY IN TREATMENT.

If the discharge turned on a timeline, I wanted the timeline. Instead, the records became their own problem.

I was later told those records would be faxed to American Health so my treatment could continue there. The records that were supposed to explain the discharge were now also the records that were supposed to make the transfer work.

I later raised the Pacific Western issues through a DHCS complaint. The complaint and oversight side of that story is documented separately on the medical-abandonment page.

See Pacific Western / DHCS Complaint Record
I STILL DO NOT KNOW WHY THE RECORDS NEVER ARRIVED.
02 / A TRANSFER THAT DID NOT TRANSFER

PACIFIC WESTERN → AMERICAN HEALTH

I was told that my records would be sent to American Health so I could transfer into treatment there.

I arrived at American Health acutely sick and in withdrawal.

I waited for the transfer records.

They never came.

An American Health counselor eventually looked at me and said, “I don't know why they are doing this to you.”

At that point American Health stopped waiting for paperwork that was not arriving. They bypassed the transfer and processed me as an entirely new intake so treatment could continue.

The missing file was not some administrative side note. It was the same file that would have contained the treatment history and the chronology behind the Pacific Western discharge.

The care moved forward without the explanation ever catching up.

THE TREATMENT CONTINUED. THE EXPLANATION DID NOT.
03 / THEN THE TREATMENT BECAME THE PROBLEM

METHADONE WAS NOT WORKING FOR ME

American Health kept me in methadone treatment, but over the following months I became physically sick while taking it and lost roughly 30 pounds.

Whatever methadone was accomplishing for the opioid dependence, I did not feel physically capable of staying on it indefinitely.

So I started tapering down because I wanted off.

That created the next problem:

HOW DO I GET FROM METHADONE TO SUBOXONE WITHOUT TURNING THE TRANSITION INTO ANOTHER MEDICAL CRISIS?
04 / NOW THE PRESCRIPTION RECORD STARTS TALKING

AUGUST 3, 2025

The story had already started. August 3 is where my CURES prescription history starts talking.

The CURES query covers March 14, 2025 through March 14, 2026. There are no controlled-substance prescription entries in that report before August 3.

On August 3, 2025, I walked into an emergency room and walked back out with buprenorphine/naloxone — Suboxone: six films, a three-day supply.

That became one of the first contradictions in the record.

The emergency room could start the medication. It could stabilize me for a few days at a time. What I could not seem to obtain was somebody willing and able to own what happened after those few days ended.

Starting a Schedule III controlled substance from a blank CURES prescription history took one emergency-room visit. Later, maintaining continuity of Schedule IV benzodiazepines while trying to get off them became an obstacle course.

THE SCHEDULE DID NOT DETERMINE ACCESS. POLICY DID.

Suboxone has a public-health infrastructure built around getting people onto treatment. Benzodiazepine dependence has no equivalent medication waiting on the other side.

The basic treatment is much less dramatic: stabilize, reduce gradually, monitor, adjust, repeat.

In other words, continuity is part of the treatment.

05 / PROGRAMS EXISTED

THE ER COULD START IT. THE TREATMENT SYSTEM COULD NOT HOLD IT.

A temporary emergency prescription was not ongoing treatment. I still needed an actual program capable of carrying the transition.

TARZANA

I tried to enroll at Tarzana Treatment Centers in Palmdale.

This distinction matters: I did not simply decide that I did not like Tarzana and walk away.

According to my recollection of the intake, my concurrent benzodiazepine taper made the case too complex and too much of a liability for the program to accept.

So I did not enroll because the program would not take the case I actually had.

THE RISK DID NOT DISAPPEAR BECAUSE A TREATMENT PROGRAM DID NOT WANT TO OWN IT.

It remained mine.

AMERICAN HEALTH — AGAIN

American Health also had a Suboxone program, so I tried that next.

The problem there was different.

The program operated much like its methadone program: daily on-site dosing. At the same time, emergency physicians had been temporarily stabilizing me with two 4 mg Suboxone films per day. My body handled the split arrangement better than taking the full amount as a single 8 mg surge.

I asked the American Health physician whether the program could accommodate that split dosing.

Her answer was:

“THAT'S NOT HOW THE PROGRAM WORKS.”

That was the distinction. The medication existed. The program existed. But the program structure was the program structure.

I declined enrollment.

On my way out, she told me, “Good luck, I hope you get better.”

I thanked her.

And laughed.

MEDICATION EXISTED. PROGRAMS EXISTED. CARE COULD STILL FAIL TO EXIST.
06 / THE REFERRAL CATCH-22

THEN I CALLED L.A. CARE

During this period I searched L.A. Care's provider directory for an addiction-medicine specialist.

I found nothing useful.

So I called the health plan.

This part is based on my recollection of the call. The surviving call log on my old phone begins later, so I am not assigning the call a date I cannot independently prove.

The representative searched the same directory.

Nothing.

The call was escalated to a supervisor.

The supervisor searched too.

Still no identifiable specialist.

Nobody on the call could tell me who the addiction-medicine physician was that I was supposedly supposed to see.

Then the supervisor told me I needed to go through my primary-care provider, obtain a note, and get a referral.

A referral to whom?

That part was never answered.

I COULD NOT FIND THE SPECIALIST. THE PLAN COULD NOT FIND THE SPECIALIST. THE SUPERVISOR COULD NOT FIND THE SPECIALIST. I STILL NEEDED A PCP REFERRAL TO THE SPECIALIST NOBODY COULD NAME.

I called primary care.

The next appointment was weeks away.

That was the point where the administrative timeline and the physiological timeline completely separated from each other.

07 / I STOPPED WAITING

THE BERNESE METHOD

By then I had already been discharged from one methadone program, started over at another, become sick on methadone, received temporary Suboxone treatment through emergency medicine, tried Tarzana, tried American Health's Suboxone program, searched the health-plan directory and called the plan itself.

There was always another procedure.

Another program.

Another referral.

Another appointment.

Another reason to wait.

Pacific Western discharge → American Health → methadone became intolerable → temporary ER Suboxone → Tarzana would not take the case → American Health's Suboxone structure did not work for me → the health plan could not identify the specialist → I did the transition myself.

Heroin was not where my opioid history began. I went from Tramadol → Oxycodone → Acetylfentanyl. Heroin entered the picture years later, when access itself had become part of the problem.

I was unfamiliar enough with heroin that at one point I got No. 3 heroin, tried to grind it into fine dust and tried to sniff it. Since I did not know it was supposed to be smoked the result was a slightly bloody nose followed by a comforting feeling of warmth and puffiness.

That detail matters because references to heroin later in this chronology can otherwise create the wrong impression. Heroin was not the beginning of the progression. I backed into it years later.

By the time I used the Bernese method, I was tapering away from black tar heroin and gunpowder heroin while gradually introducing buprenorphine from a single 8 mg Suboxone film that I physically divided into 32 pieces over the course of the transition.

I was doing, on my own, the thing the formal treatment system had repeatedly failed to provide a workable path for.

I did it myself.

No physician supervised it.

No clinic managed it.

No health plan coordinated it.

This page is not an instruction manual, so I am not reproducing the timing or a piece-by-piece dosing sequence. The point is what actually happened: I was tapering away from black tar heroin and gunpowder heroin while carrying out a 32-piece Suboxone micro-induction outside formal medical care.

THAT DOES NOT MAKE WHAT I DID SAFE. IT MAKES THE COMPARISON UGLY.

The Suboxone film that made the transition possible did not come from any of those systems either.

It came from Jose number 3.

THANKS FOR THE STRIP, JOSE NUMBER 3. YOU'RE STILL A BUM. The beef is unrelated.

I give credit where credit is due, even to bums.

The transition worked.

THE BERNESE METHOD WAS NOT PLAN A. IT WAS THE END OF A CHAIN.
08 / HARD DOCUMENTARY ANCHOR

OCTOBER 3, 2025

By October the situation was plainly inside the medical record.

Bartz-Altadonna documented that I was using Suboxone and Clonazepam, that I reported seizing without Clonazepam, and that I had been obtaining Suboxone refills through emergency rooms.

One controlled substance was being maintained through emergency medicine.

The other carried documented seizure risk if continuity failed.

THE CHART HAD A GAP. MY BODY DID NOT.
09 / THE MACHINE MAKES AN ARTIFACT

THEN A GRIEVANCE APPEARED

On November 4, 2025, L.A. Care sent me a grievance-resolution letter stating that it had received a grievance on October 5.

I had not sat down and deliberately filed one.

The letter nevertheless summarized the problem: difficulty finding an addiction specialist, providers that could not accept me or were not taking new members, and reliance on emergency rooms to keep getting medication.

I had interacted with one part of the machine, walked away, and another part of the machine generated a formal artifact describing what had happened.

I HAD ACCIDENTALLY DISCOVERED THAT YOU COULD STRESS-TEST THE SYSTEM SIMPLY BY INTERACTING WITH IT AND WATCHING WHAT PAPERWORK CAME OUT THE OTHER END.

The administrative process began documenting the gap after the gap had already forced me outside the administrative process.

10 / THE PART THAT ACTUALLY WORKED

PRIVATE OUTPATIENT TAPER

Eventually I found outpatient benzodiazepine taper management that worked.

I paid for it myself.

The provider was initially hesitant to agree to the roughly six-month taper I was asking for. He told me that benzodiazepine tapers often run much longer — roughly 12 to 18 months in the kind of long-term situation we were discussing.

After hearing my prior history and considering my age, he agreed to take me on.

Even then, the taper did not begin with diazepam alone.

It began as a cross-taper: alprazolam was reduced while diazepam was increased alongside it. After that transition, the treatment continued as a diazepam-only taper.

THIS WAS A CROSS-TAPER FIRST, THEN A DIAZEPAM TAPER.

Read chronologically, the structure is visible without interpretation gymnastics: alprazolam 3 → 1.5 → 1 mg/day while diazepam rises 10 → 15 → 20 mg/day, followed by a diazepam-only taper through 20 → 17.5 → 15 → 12.5 mg/day.

There was another odd detail sitting directly inside the same cross-taper: I paid cash for the alprazolam while the diazepam was being paid through Medicaid. Two medications. One coordinated taper. Two completely different payment paths.

THE TAPER IS VISIBLE IN THE DISPENSING RECORD ITSELF.

THE PROVIDER WHO ACTUALLY PLANNED FOR THE GAP

By May 28, I was documented as stable at 12.5 mg/day with no current interdose-withdrawal symptoms.

The treatment was working.

When I could no longer afford to keep paying out of pocket, my previous taper provider did not simply drop the treatment relationship and tell me to figure it out.

They knew what I was walking into: finding an in-network physician willing to assume an established benzodiazepine taper, with my history and concurrent Suboxone treatment, was going to be extraordinarily difficult.

So the practice continued my taper management for approximately another 30 days as grace care while I searched for an in-network replacement.

And when that additional month ran out, they still did not leave me with nothing. Active management ended June 8, and they issued the final 28-day prescription filled June 10 to give me additional runway.

I am not pretending there was no liability or continuity-of-care reason for handling the end of an established benzodiazepine taper carefully. There obviously was risk in simply cutting it off. The point is more practical than that: they recognized the transition problem before it became a crisis and actually planned for it.

Thirty additional days of care.

Then a final prescription.

Time. Medication. A runway.

What they could not manufacture was an in-network physician willing to be standing at the other end of it.

THEY DID NOT CREATE THE GAP. THEY SAW THE GAP COMING.

The final prescription was not evidence that continuity had been solved. It was evidence that my previous provider knew it had not been solved.

The medication bought more time.

It could not create a physician.

THE PREVIOUS PROVIDER BUILT THE BRIDGE AS FAR AS THEY COULD. THE NETWORK NEVER FINISHED IT.
11 / PURGATORY

REFERRAL IS NOT CARE

A referral is information.

Care is an event.

A provider name is not treatment. A phone number is not treatment. An appointment opening is not treatment. A directory listing is not treatment.

And a referral to somebody who has never reviewed the records, never accepted the patient and never agreed to assume responsibility is not continuity of care.

This is the loophole I came to think of as purgatory.

Nobody has to explicitly say no.

They can refer. Then refer again. Then provide another number. Then explain jurisdiction.

Every individual unit can appear to have completed an administrative task while the patient still has no doctor.

A DENIAL CREATES A RECORD. SILENCE CREATES DELAY. DELAY CREATES EXHAUSTION. AND EXHAUSTION IS CHEAPER THAN ACCOUNTABILITY.
12 / PAPER CLASSIFICATION VS. REALITY

INPATIENT OR NOTHING

At another point, the county substance-use system classified the situation at a level that effectively meant inpatient treatment.

My recollection of the message was simple:

Outpatient was too dangerous.

That never made much sense to me because the thing I was trying to preserve was an established outpatient taper.

Later, outpatient management was in fact accomplished.

The classification was one thing.

The physiological reality was another.

13 / WHEN THE BUFFER ENDED

JULY 2026

The final private-care medication buffer ran out around July 8.

Then the access problem stopped being administrative and became physical again.

By July 29, the medical record said that my last diazepam dose had been July 8 and that I reported emergency-department visits on July 13, 14 and 15 for withdrawal symptoms.

The same note documented that the clinician declined to provide benzodiazepines, that I had withdrawn several times, and that I was obtaining them on the street.

The plan was another referral to addiction medicine.

THE CHART DOCUMENTED WITHDRAWAL. THE CHART DOCUMENTED STREET SOURCING. THE MEDICATION WAS DECLINED. A REFERRAL WAS GENERATED.

Referral ≠ care.

The chart had a gap.

My body did not.

THE GAPS WERE THE STREETS, WHETHER I LIKED IT OR NOT.

The strange part is that, in my experience, the street never once failed to produce continuity when I needed it.

The formal healthcare system did.

That does not make the street safe. It makes the comparison uncomfortable for some to accept.

I was repeatedly warned that obtaining medication outside the medical system was reckless, dangerous and unacceptable.

But here I am.

The street carried risks everyone was eager to name.

The healthcare system carried risks everyone was eager to administratively assign somewhere else.

14 / RISK IN EVERY DIRECTION

THE DOUBLE BIND

Street "pressed" benzodiazepines were dangerous.

Prescription benzodiazepines were dangerous.

Combining benzodiazepines with Suboxone was dangerous.

Stopping benzodiazepines abruptly was dangerous.

Tapering too quickly was dangerous.

Continuing them made clinicians uncomfortable.

Interrupting them created withdrawal.

Every direction came with a warning.

What proved extraordinarily difficult to obtain was somebody willing to own the middle:

FINE. I WILL MANAGE THE RISK.

That is different from medicine not knowing what to do.

15 / ACCESS-GAP ENDPOINT

AUGUST 13, 2026

Then the supposedly complicated problem ended in the most anticlimactic way possible.

A doctor treated me.

On August 13, outpatient taper management was documented again: an actual outpatient physician visit, a diazepam treatment order at 12.5 mg per day, and scheduled follow-up care.

No inpatient admission.

No magical detox technology.

No revolutionary new medication. (Same ol' Teva 3926s) 😎

A physician assumed responsibility for outpatient management.

See Outpatient Management Endpoint

The existence of care on August 13 does not erase what happened before August 13.

It establishes the endpoint of the gap.

16 / THE SEAMS

THERE IS NO SYSTEM

Eventually I stopped thinking of all of this as one healthcare system.

There are systems:

Health plan. Primary care. Medical groups. Behavioral health. County substance-use services. Pharmacies. Medi-Cal Rx. Emergency departments. DMHC. DHCS. Licensing boards. Transportation contractors. Administrative hearing systems.

Each has its own rules, database, call center, jurisdiction and definition of responsibility.

The failures happen at the boundaries.

THE GAPS ARE THE SYSTEM.

Touch one gap and you inherit the chronology.

18 / THE PROJECT

WHAT CALAUDIT ACTUALLY IS

CalAudit does not explain how systems are supposed to work.

There are already government websites for that.

CalAudit applies pressure and records what actually happens.

A provider directory says a specialist exists? Call.

A health plan says it coordinates care? Ask it to coordinate care.

A regulator says it handles urgent complaints? Submit one.

An agency says there is a deadline? Start the clock.

A grievance says the problem is resolved? Compare the resolution against reality.

A pharmacy says insurance rejected something? Pull the claims.

CALAUDIT IS A LIVE STRESS TEST OF INSTITUTIONAL, TECHNICAL AND ADMINISTRATIVE SYSTEMS.

I eventually stopped treating contradictions as distractions.

The contradictions became the subject.

19 / NO REDEMPTION ARC REQUIRED

IF I COULD DO IT AGAIN

I eventually obtained in-network outpatient treatment almost a year after all of this nonsense began.

I learned a dangerous amount about the inner workings of Medi-Cal.

I learned that a referral does not mean somebody accepted responsibility.

I learned that a bureaucracy can document a problem with extraordinary precision while still failing to solve it.

And I built CalAudit.

I needed healthcare.

I asked the healthcare system for healthcare.

Everything after that just became more ink on paper.

WOULDA, COULDA, SHOULDA.

IT IS WHAT IT IS.
