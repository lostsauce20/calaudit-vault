KNOX-KEENE ACT CITATION

Enforcing Network Adequacy

Health insurance entities rely heavily on consumers filing vague, emotional complaints that they can easily file away and ignore. To pierce their corporate shielding, you must attack their revenue model. These health plans accept massive, lucrative monthly capitation payments from the State of California—funded entirely by tax dollars—specifically earmarked to build and maintain an adequate network of providers. When they pocket that state funding but fail to provide you with a doctor, they aren't just denying care; they are breaching their state contract.

You must anchor your internal grievances and DMHC Standard Complaints within their precise statutory licensing framework to expose this loophole:

"This health plan receives state funding to maintain an accessible, adequate network of providers under its Knox-Keene license. By failing to provide an in-network specialist, the plan has breached its statutory duty, resulting in an unlawful delay of medically necessary care. I am requesting an immediate Single Case Agreement or out-of-network authorization funded by the plan."
  • Do not say: "The insurance company is causing me stress and treating me unfairly." (This framing allows them to categorize your filing as an emotional quality-of-service grievance, shielding them from financial or regulatory consequences).
  • Do say: "The plan is pocketing public funds while breaching its statutory duty to maintain an operational provider network." (This framing targets their state compliance metrics, forcing state regulatory attorneys to open an operational network adequacy audit).

Q1: Do I have to complete an appeal with my insurance before filing a DMHC complaint?

Yes, under normal rules you must use their internal system first. However, if your plan completely ignores your grievance or issues a formal notice admitting they cannot resolve it within the state-mandated 30-day window (HSC § 1368(a) / 28 CCR § 1300.68(a)), the plan is in statutory default. You can immediately skip the rest of their timeline and file a Standard Complaint with the DMHC.

Q2: Can L.A. Care or Call The Car (CTC) put caps or mileage limits on my medical rides?

Absolutely not. California DHCS All Plan Letter 22-008 (APL 22-008) explicitly outlaws all radius limits, frequency rules, or arbitrary caps on non-emergency medical transportation (NEMT) or non-medical transportation (NMT). If an agent tells you that you are out of rides or out of bounds, they are misrepresenting state law to clear the queue.

Q3: Why did the DMHC reject my Conlan (DHCS 4521) complaint as "Out of Jurisdiction"?

This is a jurisdictional barrier. If your filing text is misclassified by intake algorithms as a pharmacy-benefit dispute or a direct financial claim that belongs to the DHCS (Medi-Cal's parent agency), the DMHC will issue an "Out of Jurisdiction" notice. This happened with the initial Conlan track. To prevent this in current filings—such as the active transportation case—ensure the complaint description is laser-focused on the administrative defaults, network adequacy failures, and statutory timeline violations of the plan itself, rather than the secondary financial reimbursement details. If you are seeking out-of-pocket medical reimbursement under Conlan v. Bonta, consult our walkthrough for how to fill out conlan claim forms.

Q4: What is the technical difference between an IMR and a Standard Consumer Complaint?

An Independent Medical Review (IMR) handles medical necessity disputes where doctors disagree on a specific treatment protocol. A Standard Consumer Complaint is handled by state enforcement attorneys and is utilized when a health plan triggers a structural network defect, fails access parameters, or blows clear statutory timelines.

Q5: What is the deadline to file a DMHC Independent Medical Review?

You have exactly 6 months from the date on your health plan's final denial letter to file an IMR or Standard Complaint with the DMHC.

Q6: Does the DMHC IMR form handle out-of-network or prior authorization denials?

No. The IMR board only reviews "Medical Necessity" denials. If your care was denied for an administrative reason—like going out-of-network, missing prior authorization, or a network adequacy failure—you must file a DMHC Standard Consumer Complaint instead to force a regulatory audit.

Q7: What if my Medi-Cal plan tells me my medication taper is a County mental health issue?

A severe physical medication taper (like Benzodiazepines) is a physical medical emergency, not just a behavioral issue. Your health plan has a legal duty of "Care Coordination." If the county cannot immediately provide a specialized medical doctor, your health plan must authorize out-of-network medical care to prevent physiological harm.

Q8: What happens if a Medi-Cal plan denies a Continuity of Care request?

If you are actively being treated for a severe condition and your plan drops your doctor, you can request "Continuity of Care" for up to 12 months. If they deny this or offer your doctor a lowball contract they know will be refused, file a DMHC Standard Complaint immediately.

Q9: What happens if L.A. Care takes longer than 30 days to resolve my transportation grievance?

They are in statutory violation of Health and Safety Code Section 1368(a) and 28 CCR Section 1300.68(a), which require plans to resolve grievances within 30 calendar days. If the plan misses the 30-day window or issues an unauthorized extension letter admitting they cannot meet the deadline, you have an immediate legal right to bypass their process and file a Standard Consumer Complaint directly with the DMHC.

Q10: Are these access issues part of a larger, systemic problem in California?

Q11: Can a public health plan require compliance with local custom pre-suit or refund procedures before I can sue?

City of Carson (decided August 10, 2026, S289952), the Government Claims Act (GCA) fully occupies the field of pre-suit claim-presentation requirements for money-or-damages actions against local public entities.
