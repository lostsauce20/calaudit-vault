THE KNOX-KEENE ACT

The California law that gives health plan members enforceable rights — and the one networks hope you never read.

// STATUTORY_DEFINITIONS: VIOLATION_METRICS

A Knox-Keene violation occurs when a California health care service plan or managed care HMO breaches the statutory mandates of the Knox-Keene Health Care Service Plan Act of 1975 (HSC § 1340 et seq.). Common violations include failing to resolve expedited clinical grievances within 72 hours (HSC § 1368.01), maintaining fraudulent "Ghost Network" provider directories (HSC § 1367.27), and failing to provide timely access to medically necessary care (28 CCR § 1300.67.2.2).

The Knox-Keene Grievance Pipeline: Enforcing Your Rights Under HSC § 1368.01

California Health & Safety Code §§ 1340–1399.818 (opens in a new window)

Most managed care members never know this law exists. That is not an accident.

Knox-Keene Act Summary

The Knox-Keene Health Care Service Plan Act of 1975 is the foundational California law that regulates HMOs and managed care health plans. Enforced by the Department of Managed Health Care (DMHC), the Act establishes strict statutory timelines for grievance processing, mandates timely access to care, and makes willful violations of patient rights a punishable offense. In summary, it is the legal framework that guarantees California patients receive timely and necessary medical services.

The Knox-Keene Health Care Service Plan Act of 1975 is California's primary law governing managed care health plans — including commercial HMOs and Medi-Cal plans like L.A. Care, Anthem Blue Cross, and Molina Healthcare. It is enforced by the Department of Managed Health Care (DMHC). For direct, step-by-step instructions on filing regulatory complaints, refer to our comprehensive dmhc guide.

Knox-Keene is not a suggestion. It is a legally binding framework that tells health plans exactly what they must do, when they must do it, and what happens when they don't. A willful violation of the Knox-Keene Act is a crime under California law.

This page documents the most critical rights Knox-Keene gives you as a healthcare member — in plain language, with the actual statutory citations, so you can use them to Audit your Health Plan.

1. The Dual-Track Grievance Timelines: Standard vs. Expedited

§ 1368.01(b) | POWERFUL RIGHT

The 72-Hour Expedited Rule (HSC 1368.01)

"Imminent threat. 72 hours. No exceptions."

▶

If your health condition presents an imminent and serious threat to your health — including severe pain, potential loss of life, loss of limb, or loss of major bodily function — your health plan is legally required to resolve your grievance on an expedited basis.

Under Health & Safety Code § 1368.01(b) (opens in a new window) and 28 CCR § 1300.68.01, the plan must inform you and the DMHC in writing of their expedited review decision — or its pending status — no later than 72 hours after receiving your grievance.

Note on Urgent Care: While most expedited grievances are 72 hours, the standard for urgent care appointments is 48 hours if no prior authorization is required, and 96 hours if prior authorization is required.

The 72-hour clock starts the moment the plan receives notice. Not when they get around to processing it. Not when a supervisor reviews it. The moment it is received.

What triggers this right: Severe pain. Risk of permanent disability. Life-threatening conditions. Situations where waiting 30 days for a standard grievance response would cause irreversible harm.

What the plan must do: Issue a written decision or written acknowledgment of pending status — to both you and the DMHC — within 72 hours. Silence is a violation.

Use the statutory timeline audit tool to calculate your exact 72-hour deadline and generate a demand letter if your plan has already defaulted.

Verbatim Statute: HSC § 1368.01(b)
"The grievance system shall allow enrollees an expedited review process... In case of a grievance involving an imminent and serious threat to the health of the enrollee, including, but not limited to, severe pain, potential loss of life, limb, or major bodily function, the health care service plan shall provide a written statement of the disposition or pending status to the enrollee and the department within 72 hours of receipt."
§ 1368.01(a) | STANDARD GRIEVANCES

The 30-Day Standard Grievance Rule

"Every complaint. Every time. 30 days."

▶

For grievances that are not time-critical, your health plan has 30 calendar days from receipt to resolve your complaint in writing. This is not a target. It is a legal ceiling.

Under Health & Safety Code § 1368.01(a) (opens in a new window) and 28 CCR § 1300.68(a), the plan's grievance system must receive, review, and resolve grievances within 30 calendar days. Every health plan is required to have a grievance process and to inform members about it. If your plan fails to respond within 30 days, that failure is itself a violation — and opens the door to DMHC complaint and Independent Medical Review.

Important: You do not need to use the word "grievance" for your complaint to be legally treated as one. Under federal regulations, if you express dissatisfaction — in writing or verbally — the plan must capture it as a grievance. They cannot talk you out of filing one.

Run your grievance dates through the audit tool to see exactly when your 30-day window closes.

Verbatim Statute: HSC § 1368.01(a)
"The grievance system shall allow enrollees to submit grievances... The system shall provide for written acknowledgment of a grievance within five calendar days of receipt... and shall resolve grievances within 30 calendar days of receipt."

2. Navigating the Jurisdictional Boundary: DMHC vs. CDI vs. ERISA

The strict grievance timelines and network mandates governed by the Knox-Keene Act do not apply to the following plan types, which operate entirely outside the regulatory scope of the California Department of Managed Health Care (DMHC):

  • Traditional PPO Plans (CDI Jurisdiction): Indemnity and standard Preferred Provider Organization products focusing on medical cost reimbursement rather than direct network care delivery are regulated under the California Insurance Code by the **California Department of Insurance (CDI)**.
  • Self-Insured Employer Plans (ERISA Exemption): Large corporate or union health plans where the employer directly shoulders financial risk are entirely exempt from California insurance mandates under federal law via the **Employee Retirement Income Security Act (ERISA)**.
  • Fee-for-Service Medi-Cal (Regular FFS): Straight Medi-Cal operating on a public fee-for-service basis completely bypasses the DMHC and answers directly to the **California Department of Health Care Services (DHCS)**.
⚠️ LITIGATION IMPACT CHECK

When auditing a health plan for timely access or grievance defaults, always verify the plan designation on the member ID card first. If the card or policy documentation indicates an ERISA Self-Insured framework or a CDI-regulated PPO, Knox-Keene statutory tools (such as the HSC § 1368.01 72-hour/30-day limits) cannot be used as a basis for formal administrative default complaints.

3. Ghost Networks & Timely Access: Securing Out-of-Network Care Under 28 CCR § 1300.67.2.2

§ 1374.30 | INDEPENDENT REVIEW

Independent Medical Review (IMR)

"An external decision the plan cannot override."

▶

If your health plan denies, modifies, or delays a service on grounds of medical necessity, you have the right to request an Independent Medical Review (IMR) from the DMHC under Health & Safety Code § 1374.30 (opens in a new window).

An IMR is conducted by an independent medical reviewer — not your plan, not your plan's doctor. If the IMR determines the denied service is medically necessary, the plan must provide it. The IMR decision is binding.

Under the Clinical Parity Act (AB 980), California mandates strict clinical parity, forcing health plans to cover the medically necessary treatment of physical conditions under the same terms and conditions as mental health services, expanding your right to an unhindered medical review.

When you can skip straight to IMR without filing a grievance first: If there is a serious threat to your health, you can request an IMR directly without exhausting the plan's internal grievance process. You do not have to wait 30 days.

How to request: Contact DMHC at 1-888-466-2219 or file at dmhc.ca.gov. It is free. There is no filing fee.

Verbatim Statute: HSC § 1374.30(a)
"Every health care service plan shall provide an independent medical review process... under which an enrollee may request an independent medical review of a decision by the plan to deny, modify, or delay health care services on the basis of medical necessity, as defined in Section 1374.305, or that the plan's clinical guidelines or criteria are not clinically appropriate."
§ 1367 | TIMELY ACCESS

Your Right to Timely Access to Care

"Coverage on paper means nothing without access in practice."

▶

Knox-Keene requires health plans to maintain provider networks that give members timely access to medically necessary care. This is codified in Health & Safety Code § 1367 (opens in a new window) and enforced through 28 CCR § 1300.67.2.2.

Specific timely access standards include:

  • Urgent care appointments: within 48 hours
  • Non-urgent primary care: within 10 business days
  • Non-urgent specialist care: within 15 business days
  • Mental health (non-physician): within 10 business days
  • Mental health follow-up: within 10 business days (SB 221)

Geographic Access: Plans must also ensure you can reach a primary care provider or hospital within 15 miles or 30 minutes of your home or workplace.

If medically necessary treatment is not available within the plan's network within these timeframes or distances, the plan must arrange out-of-network coverage at in-network cost sharing rates. They cannot charge you more because their own network failed to meet the legal standard.

Verbatim Statute: HSC § 1367(g)
"Each health care service plan shall provide health care services... and shall maintain a provider network that ensures that health care services are provided in a timely manner, consistent with professionally recognized standards of practice, geographic access standards, and time-elapsed standards."
§ 1368.04 | ENFORCEMENT

What Happens When Health Plans Violate the Law

"Administrative penalties. Civil liability. Criminal exposure."

▶

Knox-Keene violations are not without consequence — even if the DMHC's individual complaint process is slow and frustrating. Under Health & Safety Code § 1368.04 (opens in a new window), the DMHC Director has authority to assess administrative penalties against health plans for violations of the grievance system requirements.

More significantly: a willful violation of the Knox-Keene Act is a crime. Health plans and their executives can face criminal liability for deliberate non-compliance.

Civil remedies are also available. Members who suffer harm from Knox-Keene violations may have claims under California's Unfair Competition Law (Business & Professions Code § 17200) and other consumer protection statutes.

Small claims court is a viable avenue for individual members seeking compensation for specific documented violations — particularly when the plan's failure caused measurable harm or forced out-of-pocket expenses.

Verbatim Statute: HSC § 1390
"Any person who willfully violates any provision of this chapter or any rule or order on this chapter shall upon conviction be punished by a fine of not more than ten thousand dollars ($10,000) or by imprisonment in the state prison, or in a county jail for not exceeding one year..."
§ 1367.01 | OUT-OF-NETWORK REIMBURSEMENT

Out-of-Network Reimbursement Rights

"If your network failed you, the plan pays."

▶

Under Health & Safety Code § 1367.01 (opens in a new window), when a managed care plan cannot provide medically necessary services within its network — due to lack of available providers, geographic barriers, or timely access failures — the plan must cover those services out-of-network at no greater cost to the member than in-network services would require.

This means: if you were forced to go out-of-network because your plan's network was inadequate, inaccessible, or failed to respond in time, you are entitled to reimbursement. The plan's administrative failures do not become your financial liability.

Reimbursement requests should be documented in writing, submitted with supporting medical records, and tracked with certified mail or other delivery confirmation. If the plan fails to process your reimbursement claim within 30 days, that is an additional Knox-Keene violation.

Verbatim Regulation: 28 CCR § 1300.67.2.2(d)(7)
"In the event that a plan cannot provide services in-network within the timely access standards... the plan shall arrange for the provision of specialty services out-of-network with cost sharing no greater than what the enrollee would pay for in-network services."
APL 25-006 | TELEPHONE ACCESS

Your Right to Be Heard (Wait Times)

"No more endless hold music."

▶

Under APL 25-006 (opens in a new window), managed care plans must meet strict telephone responsiveness standards. This is a critical component of access to care.

• Member Services: Average wait time must not exceed 10 minutes during normal business hours.

• 24/7 Triage: If you leave a message, a licensed professional must call you back within 30 minutes.

If you are routinely placed on hold for 20, 30, or 60 minutes, the plan is failing its regulatory benchmarks. Document wait times with screenshots of your call logs.

Verbatim APL 25-006 Section IV
"Managed care plans must maintain telephone systems capable of monitoring wait times... The average telephone wait time for a customer service representative during normal business hours must not exceed 10 minutes. A triage or screening service wait time must not exceed 30 minutes, 24 hours a day, 7 days a week."
§ 1367.27 | DIRECTORY ACCURACY

The Accuracy Mandate (Ghost Networks)

"A directory full of ghosts is a violation of law."

▶

Under Health & Safety Code § 1367.27 (opens in a new window), health plans are required to maintain accurate and up-to-date provider directories. This isn't just an administrative chore; it is a legal requirement designed to prevent "Ghost Networks."

Plans must verify the accuracy of their directory at least annually, and they must update their online directories at least weekly. If a directory contains incorrect addresses (like the "Ghost Office" at 1055 W. 7th St.) or lists providers who are not actually accepting patients, the plan is in violation.

Under 2026 standards established by AB 280, the DMHC enforces a strict provider directory accuracy threshold of 60% (escalating to 95% by 2029) and imposes direct civil penalties on plans that fail to maintain active, validated provider phone numbers and physical locations.

Your Right: If you rely on an inaccurate directory and suffer financial harm or a delay in care, you can document the inaccuracy as a Knox-Keene violation. Accurate directories are the baseline for "Timely Access."

Verbatim Statute: HSC § 1367.27(a)
"A health care service plan shall publish and maintain a provider directory or directories... and shall update its online directory at least weekly, ensuring that directory listings are accurate, up-to-date, and complete to prevent barriers to enrollee timely access."
§ 1370 | QUALITY OF CARE

The Quality of Care Standard

"Medically necessary. Clinically appropriate. No exceptions."

▶

Under Health & Safety Code § 1370 (opens in a new window), every health plan must have a system of quality assurance to ensure that the care provided to members meets professionally recognized standards of practice.

This means the plan cannot prioritize corporate "utilization management" over clinical safety. If a plan denies a treatment that is medically necessary according to peer-reviewed clinical guidelines, they are violating the standard of care mandate. For patients on high-risk protocols (like Benzodiazepine tapers), the "standard of care" is a legal requirement for institutional safety.

The Audit View: When a plan defaults on a 72-hour grievance for a life-critical condition, they aren't just missing a deadline — they are failing to meet the basic Quality of Care standards required to hold a Knox-Keene license in California.

Verbatim Statute: HSC § 1370
"Every plan shall establish procedures... to assure that the quality of care provided enrollees meets professionally recognized standards of practice... systems of clinical peer review and continuous medical audit shall be implemented to ensure optimal clinical outcomes and safety."

5. What To Do If Your Plan Violates Knox-Keene

Document Everything. Move In Writing. Know Your Timeline.

Step 1 — File a written grievance immediately. Do not rely on phone calls. Put your complaint in writing. Use certified mail with return receipt. Note the date and time you submit it. This starts the 30-day or 72-hour clock — plug that date into the audit tool to track your deadline automatically.

Step 2 — Invoke the 72-hour rule explicitly. If your situation involves imminent harm, state clearly in your grievance that you are invoking your rights under Health & Safety Code § 1368.01(b) and requesting expedited review. Use the statute number. Plans respond differently to members who know the law.

Step 3 — Document the silence. If the plan misses the 72-hour or 30-day deadline, document it. Screenshot. Save emails. Use USPS Informed Delivery to prove nothing arrived. A missed deadline is itself a violation and becomes part of your evidence record.

Step 4 — Request an Independent Medical Review. If your denial involves medical necessity, request an IMR from the DMHC immediately. It is free. It is binding. It bypasses the plan entirely.

Step 5 — Consider small claims court. For documented violations with measurable financial harm, California small claims court (limit: $12,500) is accessible without an attorney. The plan's violation of a specific statutory requirement is your cause of action.

The Knox-Keene Act gives you real legal weapons. The only question is whether you know how to use them.

FAQ

6. Frequently Asked Questions: Knox-Keene Act & HSC 1368.01

▶ What is the 72-hour expedited rule under HSC 1368.01?
Under Health & Safety Code § 1368.01(b), if a health condition presents an imminent and serious threat to your health, the health plan must inform you and the DMHC of its decision or pending status no later than 72 hours after receiving the grievance.
▶ How long does a health plan have to respond to a standard grievance?
Under Health & Safety Code § 1368.01(a), health plans have 30 calendar days from receipt to resolve a standard grievance in writing.
▶ What are the timely access standards for appointments?
Standards include: Urgent care within 48 hours, non-urgent primary care within 10 business days, and non-urgent specialist care within 15 business days.
▶ Can I sue my health plan for violating Knox-Keene?
Yes. Under California's Unfair Competition Law (Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200) and through small claims court (up to $12,500), individual members can seek damages for documented violations. A willful violation is also a crime under the Knox-Keene Act itself.
▶ What's the difference between a grievance and an appeal?
A grievance is any complaint about a health plan's actions or inactions. An appeal is a specific request to reconsider a denial, modification, or delay of a service. Under HSC 1368.01, both are subject to the 30-day standard timeline, but expedited appeals (urgent denials) trigger the 72-hour rule.
▶ Does Knox-Keene apply to fee-for-service Medi-Cal?
No. The Knox-Keene Act applies only to managed care health plans (like L.A. Care, Health Net, Anthem, Molina, IEHP, Kaiser). Fee-for-service Medi-Cal is governed by different regulations under DHCS authority.
