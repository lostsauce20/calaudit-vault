Understanding the Government Claims Act (GCA)

Navigating litigation against a California public entity (such as L.A. Care Health Plan) requires strict compliance with the Government Claims Act (often mistakenly referred to as the "Government Tort Claims Act"). The California Supreme Court adopted the term Government Claims Act (GCA) because pre-suit presentation requirements reach beyond torts and include statutory reimbursement and breach-of-contract claims (see City of Stockton v. Superior Court). Two primary statutory provisions govern this administrative process:

§ 905 — The Presentation Mandate & Exemptions Government Code § 905 is the general presentation rule specifying that all claims for money or damages against local public entities must be presented under the statutory procedure. While § 905(e) expressly excepts applications or claims for public assistance (such as Medi-Cal benefits/reimbursement), local health plans aggressively litigate this classification. The most bulletproof, lowest-risk approach is dual-track preservation: submitting a timely, protective Government Claim to a designated § 915 recipient, explicitly stating presentation is made protectively without waiving any § 905(e) exceptions.
§ 910 — The Data & Content Requirements Government Code § 910 dictates the strict data-points and content requirements that the presented claim must actually contain to be legally valid. Failure to satisfy any of these required data fields can result in immediate rejection or a defense of "insufficient claim presentation":
  • The name and post office address of the claimant.
  • The post office address to which the claimant desires notices to be sent.
  • The date, place, and other circumstances of the occurrence or transaction which gave rise to the claim.
  • A general description of the indebtedness, obligation, injury, damage or loss incurred so far as it may be known.
  • The name or names of the public employee or employees causing the injury, damage, or loss, if known.
  • The dollar amount claimed if it is under $10,000 (including the basis of computation). For claims over $10,000, the claim must state whether it is a limited civil case.
Preemption of Local "Extra Exhaustion" Procedures (Tesoro) On August 10, 2026, the California Supreme Court issued its landmark unanimous ruling in Tesoro Refining & Marketing Co. LLC v. City of Carson (S289952). The Court held that the Government Claims Act fully occupies the field of claim-presentation requirements for money-or-damages claims against local public entities, preempting any duplicative, locally-invented refund or administrative pre-suit procedures. Public plans like L.A. Care cannot superimpose their own custom pre-suit ordinances or internal "pre-claim" appeal steps over the GCA.
Small Claims Court Jurisdiction & Monetary Ceilings Suit under the GCA can be brought in Small Claims Court (Form SC-100). Under Code of Civil Procedure § 116.221, the individual small claims ceiling for natural persons is $12,500. Although public entities are subject to a lower $6,250 ceiling *when they are filing as claimants*, an individual natural person suing a public health authority retains the full $12,500 limit.

// Government Claims FAQ

Government Claims Act FAQ

[01] Do I need a lawyer to file a Government Claim?
[A]: No. GC § 910 is a self-service administrative requirement. The form, the certified mail receipt, and the 45-day clock are all you need to satisfy exhaustion. A lawyer is not required.
[02] What happens if the agency ignores my claim?
[A]: Silence is a win. Under GC § 912.4, if the public entity fails to act within 45 days, the claim is automatically "deemed rejected by operation of law." That rejection is your green light to file suit — no response letter required.
[03] Does filing a Government Claim cancel my DMHC complaint or small claims case?
[A]: No. These are parallel tracks. A Government Claim is a jurisdictional prerequisite for Superior Court — it doesn't affect your DMHC grievance rights, your ability to file in Small Claims, or any ongoing administrative proceedings. You can run all three simultaneously.
[04] What does the § 945.6 six-month window actually mean?
[A]: It means you have exactly six months from the date the rejection letter was "personally delivered or deposited in the mail" to file a court action on the rejected portions of your claim. For this case, L.A. Care's rejection letter was transmitted July 13, 2026 — making the hard deadline January 13, 2027. Miss that window and you lose the right to sue on those claims permanently, regardless of how strong your evidence is. File early. Don't test the deadline.
[05] What happens if DHCS never responds to a Conlan reimbursement claim?
[A]: After the 120-day statutory adjudication window expires with no response, the claim enters administrative default. At that point a petition for writ of mandate under California Code of Civil Procedure § 1085 becomes available — a court order compelling the public agency to perform its ministerial duty to adjudicate. DHCS cannot simply ignore a Conlan claim indefinitely. The clock is a legal instrument, not a suggestion.
